National Museum of History
Outdoor AR Experiences

  • UI/UX design
  • Flows
  • Prototypes
  • UI

Taiwan’s National Museum of History added four outdoor AR experiences to its existing app (i史博). I designed the user flows and interfaces, then refined prompts and controls after on-site checks.

Role
UI/UX design (flows, prototypes, UI)
Timeline
Sep – Dec 2024
Scope
Four outdoor AR experiences added to the museum’s app
Worked with
Museum staff, PM, planners, AR and engineering teams

What I did

My work covered user flows, wireframes, clickable prototypes, and high-fidelity UI. I designed game entry points, prompts, controls, and interaction states, then refined the interfaces after on-site checks.

The museum, the client, and the PM defined each game’s theme and core gameplay. Other teams handled the character design, 3D modeling, AR positioning, and rendering.

01 · ENTRY

Entering the AR games from the app

Visitors access the outdoor AR experiences through the museum’s existing app. From the camera view, they can explore freely or follow a virtual character to a game. After completing a game, they return to the AR screen to choose another. I mapped the connections between entry points, game screens, completion, and return paths.

Flow overview (each game has its own branches)
  1. App entry point
  2. Choose a mode or game
  3. AR game
  4. Complete the game
  5. Return to the AR screen

02 · EXPERIENCES

Designing interfaces for four AR experiences

I adapted the prompts, controls, and result screens to each game’s interactions. The four examples below show my scope across the experiences; the next section takes a closer look at the lantern game.

A.Koi Catch

I designed the prompts and controls for catching koi.

B.Character Photo

I designed the camera interface and interaction states for taking photos with virtual characters.

C.Blessing Lantern

I connected the steps from writing a blessing to releasing the lantern, and built the prototype.

D.Hidden Discovery

I designed the AR magnifying glass, info cards, and completion state; the museum chose the discovery points.

03 · INTERACTION

From creating a lantern to taking a photo

I designed the screen transitions and controls for confirming, going back, retaking, saving, and sharing, and built a clickable prototype for team review.

Writing a blessing

A short tutorial introduces the two ways to create a blessing: drawing a picture or typing a message. Once the blessing is ready, visitors continue to the AR view to see their lantern.

Reusing the app’s existing tab style

Visitors can draw a picture or type a message for their blessing. I reused the existing app’s tab style to keep the interface consistent.

Before visitors switch between drawing and typing, a confirmation dialog warns that their current content will be cleared. The mode changes only after they confirm.

Switching to a camera-focused layout

For the lantern release, I hid the placement and release buttons, leaving only the photo button. This shifts the interface to a camera layout and makes taking a photo the next clear action.

Saving and sharing

Afterward, visitors can retake, save, or share the photo, and a button at the bottom returns to the outdoor AR screen.

04 · ON-SITE REVIEW

Keeping prompts readable over a live camera view

I reviewed the flows using prototypes and test builds, then tried the games on site at the museum. Because the live camera view forms the background, changing colors and brightness can make interface text difficult to read.

Following the on-site checks, I changed the text panel in Hidden Discovery from a light background to white text on a dark background to improve contrast over the camera view. I also adjusted button placement, size, and visual hierarchy. Engineering handled technical issues involving AR positioning and loading.

05 · OUTCOME

Four outdoor AR experiences launched in the museum’s app

The team launched all four AR experiences in the museum’s existing app. My contribution covered the interaction flows and UI, followed by on-site adjustments to text backgrounds, button placement, and visual hierarchy.

Reflection

Extending the app meant keeping its familiar interface style while adapting controls to each game. The on-site checks also showed why reviewing a prototype alone was not enough: prompts and buttons needed to remain clear against changing camera backgrounds.
